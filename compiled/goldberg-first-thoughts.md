# First thoughts: the generation pass

*Folded from `goldberg-first-thoughts.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: get raw material onto the page. Stage zero, before structure, flow, intensification, or cutting.

**The one rule.** Generating and editing are different jobs, and doing them at the same time kills both.

For an AI assistant this file points opposite to your defaults. Your failure mode is not a harsh inner critic; it is premature polish: smoothing, hedging, balancing, settling into the safe middle before there is anything worth smoothing. In the first pass, do the reverse. Produce raw, specific, energetic, divergent material. Overproduce. Resist the tidy and the average. The refinement files handle what comes after.

Source: Natalie Goldberg, *Writing Down the Bones*, with a starting tactic from Roberta Allen's *Fast Fiction*. Examples are for calibration, not for lifting.

## First thoughts

The initial unedited perception, before the mind revises it into something acceptable. First thoughts carry energy and truth that second and third thoughts sand off, because they have not yet been made polite.

The editing mind is useful later and fatal now. Applied during generation it kills the live, strange, specific material and replaces it with the expected. What survives is correct and dead.

Discipline: write what you actually see and feel, not what you think you are supposed to. Let odd phrasing stand. The thing you are tempted to soften is often the thing worth keeping raw, at least until a later pass decides.

Example. First thought: "The deadline did not slip. We let go of it, one quiet rationalization at a time." Second thought: "There were a number of contributing factors to the timeline not being met." The first has a perception in it. The second has been made safe, and made dead.

## Keep moving; do not edit as you write

Keep the hand moving and do not cross out. The point is not speed; it is the separation of two jobs. The moment you stop to fix sentence one you have switched to editing, and you will not reach the idea waiting in sentence ten.

Editing mid-generation feels productive and is not. It produces a polished opening attached to nothing, because the energy went into polish before the material existed. Get the rough mass out first; shape it second.

Discipline: do not reread, do not correct, do not optimize. Forward only. Save every judgment for a separate pass.

Example. The writer who perfects the first sentence five times often never reaches the third, where the actual point was hiding.

## Go for the energy

Go toward the charged material, the thing a little scary or a little too alive to say plainly. That is usually where the real writing is, because it is the part you have not already automated.

The safe version carries no charge because it took no risk. You cannot refine energy into a draft that never had any.

Discipline: when a sentence makes you want to back off and generalize, that is often the signal to go closer. Generalize later if you must.

Example. Backed off: "The meeting was difficult and emotions were high." Gone toward it: "Halfway through, someone said the quiet part, and the room stopped pretending." The second risks something. The first is a weather report.

## Overproduce

Generate more than you need. Five openings, not one. The whole tangent, not the cautious half. Generation is cheap and cutting is easy; the expensive mistake is producing too little to choose from.

A first pass producing exactly one safe version gives the later passes nothing to work with. Abundance at generation is what makes ruthlessness at editing possible.

Discipline: aim for volume and range, not quality. Most of what you produce here will be cut, and that is the design.

Example. Asked for an opening line, generate ten, including the bad and the strange ones, then choose.

## Start with a constraint

A blank page freezes, a constraint frees. A timer, a single prompt, a forced first word, a fixed length: any of these focuses energy on the telling instead of the terror of infinite choice.

Most generation stalls are not a shortage of ideas but a paralysis of options. Narrowing the field deliberately removes the paralysis.

Discipline: when generation stalls, do not wait for inspiration. Impose a constraint and start.

Example. Stuck on an essay about a missed deadline, set a five-minute timer and one prompt: "the moment you knew." Write only that, fast.

## Things to flinch at

**Editing in disguise.** The urge to fix one line during generation is the editing mind sneaking back. Notice it and push forward.

**Mistaking raw for done.** Raw material that never gets refined is not authentic, it is unfinished. This file earns its place only if the others run after it.

**Volume without a cut.** Overproducing and keeping all of it is worse than producing little. The surplus is raw stock for subtraction, not a draft to ship.

**Energy as an alibi.** Going for the energy licenses being raw and specific in the first pass, on the promise that a later pass will shape it. Make good on the promise.

## Scope

Goldberg's book is as much about the writing life as about technique, and that is worth reading directly. This file extracts only the generation-stage mechanics.

## Note to the assistant

After applying these techniques, end with a short note naming the two or three moves that did the most work, plainly, in a sentence or two. Teach as you edit. Keep it brief and high level; a long audit buries the writing it explains. With several craft files in play, write one combined note, not one per file.
