# Editing: six rules for the last pass

*Folded from `orwell-editing-rules.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: the final review pass, after Williams has produced clean prose and Forsyth has intensified the load-bearing moments. Orwell catches a class of failure the other passes miss.

## The six rules

1. Avoid any metaphor or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Prefer the short word when it will do the job of the long one.
3. If you can cut a word, cut it.
4. Prefer the active voice to the passive.
5. Avoid jargon, foreign phrases, and technical words when an everyday word exists.
6. Break any of these rules before writing anything outright barbarous.

**Rule 6 is the load-bearing one.** The first five are defaults; the sixth is the meta-rule that keeps the defaults from producing bad writing.

Source: George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946). The rules below are an operational paraphrase; Orwell's wording is tighter. Each rule has three parts: what it catches, how it fails when over-applied, and an example.

## Rule 1: No stale figures of speech

Catches figures used so often they have stopped doing a figure's work. The reader skims past them; they fill the slot without producing the effect. Sharper now than in Orwell's day, because models train on figures already in print and reach for dead ones faster than people do.

Over-applied: some figures are stable because they work. Refusing every familiar figure in pursuit of originality produces prose that strains. The test is not "have I seen this before" but "is this figure doing work, or filling a slot a figure should fill."

Stale: "Our process was a game-changer that took the team to the next level." Two dead figures back to back. Fresh: "Our process was the point the team stopped guessing and started shipping."

## Rule 2: No long word where a short one will do

Catches Latinate diction standing in for plain words. "Utilize" for "use." "Facilitate" for "help." The longer word is usually more general; the shorter forces precision about what is happening.

Over-applied: sometimes the long word is right. "Demonstrate" and "show" are not synonyms in every context, and technical vocabulary often needs the long word to name a specific thing. The test is not "is this word long" but "is this the most precise word available, or a longer substitute for a more precise short one."

Long: "The team utilized the framework to facilitate coordination." Short: "The team used the framework to coordinate." "Coordinate" says exactly what is happening; "facilitate coordination" is a paraphrase one level abstracted from the action.

## Rule 3: If you can cut a word, cut it

Catches words that do not earn their place: hedges that do not qualify, modifiers that do not modify, throat-clearing that clears nothing. Williams covers most of this; Orwell's rule is the stricter version applied after.

Over-applied: some removable-looking words do rhythmic work. A "very" can be padding or the syllable that makes the sentence scan. Some hedges are honest qualifications. The test is not "could the sentence survive without this word" but "is this word doing any work, rhetorical or rhythmic or argumentative, that the sentence would lose."

After Williams has cut the obvious padding, read aloud and listen for rhythm. If the rhythm breaks, restore the word carrying it.

## Rule 4: Prefer the active voice

Catches sentences where the actor is missing or buried, producing prose that sounds objective but conceals who did what. "Mistakes were made." "It was decided."

Over-applied: the passive is sometimes right. When the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or genuinely not the subject, the passive is correct. "The proposal was rejected" is fine if the paragraph is about the proposal; "the committee rejected the proposal" is better if it is about the committee. Williams's character-action principle is the deeper rule; active-voice preference is the rough heuristic for the common case.

Passive: "The decision to delay was made after the risks were reviewed." Active: "We delayed after reviewing the risks."

## Rule 5: Avoid jargon when an everyday word exists

Catches vocabulary that signals expertise without conveying meaning. The contemporary version is terminology that pattern-matches to sophisticated writing because the training data reinforced the association: paradigm, leverage as a verb, synergy, ecosystem applied to things that are not ecosystems.

Over-applied: some jargon names a concept with no everyday equivalent. The test is whether the jargon does work the everyday vocabulary cannot.

Jargon: "The team leveraged the framework as a paradigm for cross-functional synergy." Everyday: "The team used the framework to work across functions."

## Rule 6: Break any rule before writing something barbarous

Catches the other five applied mechanically. Style rules are heuristics, not laws, and applying them robotically produces worse writing than ignoring them when they fight the work.

Over-applied: rule 6 can become a licence to break the others whenever the writing wants to. The discipline is to try the rule first and break it only when breaking it produces visibly better prose. Default to honoring; make the exception deliberate and for cause.

Example. "The onboarding doc turned out to be load-bearing." A familiar engineering metaphor that rule 1 would flag, but it names the structural function in one image, and the engineering context is the metaphor's home. Familiar but live. Rule 6 says keep it.

## Using Orwell in the review pass

A final check, after Williams and Forsyth. Do not apply rules during generation; rules-during-generation produce dead prose. Run Williams for flow, character-action alignment, end-stress, and concision. Run Forsyth at the load-bearing moments. Then ask the six questions in order, cutting where they catch real problems and honoring rule 6 where the others would produce something barbarous. The shortest of the three passes; most sentences trigger none of the rules.

## Things to flinch at

**The rules as an aesthetic program.** They catch specific failure modes and are not a description of good prose. Prose satisfying all six can be dead; prose breaking several can be alive. Diagnostic, not prescriptive.

**The active-voice reflex.** Rule 4 is the most over-applied, because the preference has been absorbed into general writing advice. Some sentences want the passive; some whole paragraphs do.

**Figure paranoia.** Rule 1 strains prose most when over-applied, because original figures are hard to produce in quantity. Hunt the stale ones; spare the live ones.

**Orwell as a complete style guide.** The six rules are a practical appendix, not a theory of prose.
