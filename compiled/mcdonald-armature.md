# The armature: the point a piece is built to prove

*Folded from `mcdonald-armature.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: find the single claim a piece exists to prove, and make everything serve it.

**The one question.** What is this piece built to prove, and does every part of it serve that?

Four linked ideas, not a sequence of passes: the armature, building from it, because over and, and respect for the audience. One discipline, applied when you plan and again every time you cut. The armature sits above structure: Truby gives the arc, this gives the point the arc exists to prove.

Source: Brian McDonald, *Invisible Ink*. The framework is McDonald's; the non-fiction extraction, diagnostics, and examples are the writer's. Examples are for calibration, not for lifting.

## The armature

A sculptor building a clay figure first builds an armature, a wire skeleton underneath. The viewer never sees it; it holds the figure up. Every piece worth reading has one: a single controlling idea, the one thing the piece exists to prove. Not the topic. The point.

State it as one sentence: "This piece is built to prove that ______." If you cannot fill the blank, the piece has no armature, and no amount of good prose will fix that. Not "this piece is about deadlines" but "the real cost of a missed deadline is the trust it spends, not the time." A topic is a subject; an armature is a claim.

The armature is what makes a piece feel like it is about something rather than merely on something. Without one a piece can be clear, well-structured, pleasant, and still leave the reader wondering what the point was.

Example. Topic: onboarding. Armature: "a bad first week costs a company for a year." The first is a subject you could write ten unrelated paragraphs about. The second is a claim every paragraph can serve.

## Build from the armature out

Everything is built to support it: examples, structure, asides, scenes. You arrive at the figure by removing everything that is not the figure.

The test: for every paragraph, example, and digression, does this reinforce the armature? If not, it is not neutral, it is dilution. The most common way good writers weaken a piece is keeping material they love that does not serve the point.

The hardest cuts are the good ones: the well-written paragraph, the example you are proud of. If it does not serve the armature it pulls attention off the spine. Cut it, or move it to a piece whose armature it does serve.

Example. A draft arguing a missed deadline spends trust includes a strong paragraph on the team's new project-tracking tool. The paragraph is good and does not serve an armature about trust. Cutting it removes a distraction.

## Because, not and

Children tell stories as a string of ands: we got up, and got dressed, and went to the store. Real stories and real arguments run on because. Causation, not sequence.

A piece connected by and is a list of things that happened; a piece connected by because is an argument that something follows. The reader feels it as the difference between "and then" and "which means." Because is what makes a sequence feel like it had to go the way it went.

The test: read the joints between sections. Where you find an and that should be a because, you have usually found a place where the causal link is missing or unstated.

Example. And: "We set the date, and we hit a snag, and the vendor was late, and we slipped, and the client was annoyed." Because: "We slipped because no one actually owned the date. The client's annoyance was not about the lost week. It was about discovering that no one had been holding it."

## Respect the audience

Two things at once. The burden is on the writer: if the audience does not understand, that is the writer's failure. And the audience is intelligent, and a piece works partly because the reader assembles it themselves. The reconciliation: be clear without over-explaining. Give the reader the pieces and trust them to make the connection.

Over-explanation insults the reader and kills the pleasure of recognition. The most resonant moment is usually the one the reader completes themselves; spell it out and you take that moment from them. But obscurity is not respect. Make the conclusion reachable, then let the reader reach it.

The test: find the sentence where you stated the lesson outright. Has the piece earned it? If yes, cut the statement. If no, the problem is upstream.

Example. Over-explained: "This shows that trust, once spent, is hard to rebuild, and that clarity of ownership matters on any team." Trusted: "The client did not ask for the week back. They asked who was in charge."

## Things to flinch at

**Not a moral.** "Be honest" is a slogan; "the real cost of a missed deadline is the trust it spends" is a claim with an edge. An armature that flattens into a fortune-cookie line produces a piece that reads as one.

**Not the thesis statement.** It is the idea the piece is built to prove, not necessarily a sentence in the piece. The strongest pieces often never state it outright.

**The subtraction test can over-prune.** Not every good line must argue the armature directly. Texture, voice, and a little air are part of why a reader stays. Cut what distracts, not everything that is not the thesis.

**Respect can tip into obscurity.** Trusting the reader is not hiding the point. If no one can find the armature, you have abandoned the audience rather than respected them.

## Relation to the other files

Nested with Williams: Williams asks whether every sentence serves its paragraph; the armature asks whether every paragraph serves the piece. Same discipline, one level up. You can build a perfectly shaped arc that proves nothing, which is why the armature comes first.

## Scope

McDonald's book also covers a five-part structural model, character, dialogue, and a longer treatment of audience participation. The armature is the load-bearing idea and the one that travels furthest outside fiction.
