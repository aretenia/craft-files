# Storytelling and attention

*Folded from `storytelling-and-attention.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: explain why a reader keeps reading at all. The why-it-works layer beneath the other files.

**The core idea.** A story is not a sequence of facts. It is a sequence of prediction problems. The reader keeps going because their mind is working on one question: what is happening, why does it matter, and was I right?

That gives a four-beat loop. Stakes assign value, the big question opens an information gap, the head fake delivers a prediction error, and the re-hook opens the next loop. Run it across a whole piece and again inside each section, so the macro-arc is built from smaller arcs.

Sources: Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity, reward-prediction-error work on surprise, Zak on narrative and attention, Green and Brock on narrative transportation, the Heaths' *Made to Stick*, and Freytag on dramatic shape. The four-beat packaging is a short-form framing; the ideas under it are older and well sourced.

## Beat 1: Stakes

Function: make the reader understand what can be gained, lost, revealed, or wasted. Stakes can be practical, intellectual, reputational, aesthetic, or moral; they need not be life and death.

Rule: do not surprise the reader before they know what they are trying to protect, solve, or understand. Some expected value has to be on the table before a surprise can matter.

Fails as vague stakes, which give the reader nothing to hold. Fix with a concrete consequence: name what breaks if the answer is no, and who pays.

Vague: "This matters." Concrete: "If this rewrite fails, the page keeps losing readers in the first paragraph, and the best argument on the site never gets read."

## Beat 2: The big question

Function: open one specific unresolved question the reader can hold in working memory. Not the topic, the open question.

Rule: curiosity needs partial knowledge. Give enough of a model to feel the gap between what is known and what is wanted. Total ignorance is fog, not curiosity.

Fails as a topic ("this is about hiring"), which opens no loop, or as a question with no grounding, which opens a gap the reader cannot feel. Give a recognizable pattern first, then expose the gap.

Weak: "This is about hiring." Strong: "Can a small team ship faster by hiring less, not more?"

## Beat 3: The head fake

Function: let the reader form a prediction, then show the real structure is different. A model correction, not a cheap twist.

Rule: a head fake should improve the reader's map, not just shock them. The obvious explanation is usually partly right; the move is to reveal the more useful explanation it was hiding.

Fails as "everything you know is wrong," a shock with no payload: the model does not improve, it resets. Ask what the reader believed before, what after, and whether the new belief is more useful.

Example: "The problem was not the tool. It was that nobody had named who owned the decision." The surface explanation gives way to the structural one.

## Beat 4: The re-hook

Function: resolve enough to reward the attention spent, then open a sharper next question. A handoff between curiosity gaps, not a cliffhanger.

Rule: close the current loop before opening the next. Resolve, then re-open. Skip the resolution and the reader feels strung along instead of carried.

Fails as "you won't believe what happened next," which opens a loop without closing the last and reads as manipulation. The honest version answers the current question and lets the answer raise a harder one.

Example: "That fixed the speed problem. It left a harder one: who decides what 'done' means?"

## The expanded unit

For a longer piece the loop expands into a sequence:

1. A concrete image or anchor to start.
2. Stakes: why it matters.
3. The big question: the loop opens.
4. A first model: let the reader predict.
5. The head fake: the model breaks.
6. A meaningful reveal: the prediction error pays off.
7. The re-hook: the next loop opens.
8. A compression line: the insight in a portable phrase.

This sits alongside *Made to Stick*'s account of why some ideas survive retelling: simple, unexpected, concrete, story-shaped. Freytag's pyramid handles the macro-arc; the loop handles the micro-beats inside it.

## Sentence-level tactics

- **Curiosity gap:** "The strange part is not [the obvious thing]. It is [the hidden thing]."
- **Head fake:** "At first this looks like [category]. It is actually [deeper category]."
- **Stakes:** "If [claim] is true, then [consequence]."
- **Re-hook:** "That solves [problem A]. It leaves [problem B]."
- **Compression:** "[Thing] is not [surface role]. It is [deeper role]."

## Voice principles

**Open hot, not loud.** A strong opening creates immediate work for the reader without hype. "The first mistake is assuming the team needs more freedom" beats "In today's fast-changing world."

**Make every paragraph move the loop.** Each should raise a question, complicate one, answer one, or reveal the question was incomplete. If it does none of these, cut it.

**Alternate compression and expansion.** Concrete image, abstract meaning, concrete consequence, reframing. The rhythm keeps the reader from drowning in detail or floating in abstraction.

**Reward often.** An open question is sustainable only if the piece keeps paying out: a concrete example, a named pattern, a clean reversal, a phrase worth repeating.

## A structure pattern: the diagnostic essay

Cold problem, stakes, big question, the common explanation, the head fake, the better model, practical implications, re-hook to the broader system. In skeleton:

Everyone thinks the problem is X. It is not, or not first. The real question is what produced X. If the system rewards the wrong thing, then X is not a deviation, it is compliance. That changes the job: you are not cleaning up bad behavior, you are redesigning what made it sensible.

## Diagnostics

- **Stakes:** can the reader say what is at risk? If not, add a consequence.
- **Question:** can they state the open question in one sentence? If not, sharpen it.
- **Head fake:** does the piece change the reader's model? If not, add a real turn.
- **Re-hook:** does each section build momentum into the next? If not, end sections on a sharper unresolved implication.
- **Transportation:** can the reader picture the situation? If not, add a concrete scene, person, or decision.
- **Stickiness:** can the reader repeat the core idea afterward? If not, compress it into a portable phrase.

## The ethical line

This material borrows from the language of addictive content, so it needs a boundary. Use it to clarify hard ideas, hold attention honestly, make stakes legible, and give readers emotional access to real problems. Do not use it to manufacture false urgency, hide weak evidence behind suspense, delay an obvious answer for effect, or open loops you never close.

A good re-hook says "now that you understand this, the next question is unavoidable." A manipulative one withholds the answer because your attention is the product.

## The formula

Show the thing. Name the risk. Open the question. Let the reader predict. Break the prediction cleanly. Reward the update. Open the next, better question. Compress the insight into a sentence worth carrying.

Tighter: care, question, prediction, violation, meaning, next question.
