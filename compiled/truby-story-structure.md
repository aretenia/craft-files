# Story structure: seven steps

*Folded from `truby-story-structure.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: map an argument's arc. Run it during the structural pass, before any sentence-level work.

The seven steps in Truby's order, with their non-fiction mapping:

1. Weakness and need: the gap in the reader's current understanding.
2. Desire: the question the reader brings.
3. Opponent: the view the piece argues against.
4. Plan: the piece's argumentative strategy.
5. Battle: the central, hardest argumentative moment.
6. Self-revelation: the reader's moment of recognition.
7. New equilibrium: the understanding the reader carries away.

Source: John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007). The framework is Truby's; the non-fiction adaptation, diagnostics, and examples are the writer's. The adaptation is not strict: Truby is the source of the vocabulary and the application differs.

Each entry has the same shape: Truby's term, the adaptation, the diagnostic, an illustration. One illustration runs throughout, an essay arguing that a small team ships faster by hiring less, so the arc assembles as you read.

## Step 1: Weakness and need

Truby: the hero begins with a flaw that holds them back and a need the story will surface.

Adaptation: the reader begins with an incomplete or incorrect understanding. The weakness is the gap in their current frame; the need is the deeper understanding the piece provides.

Diagnostic: can you name what the reader does not understand, and why their frame is failing them? If the answer is only "they do not know about this topic," the piece is informational, not argumentative. An argument requires a failure in the frame, not an absence of information.

Illustration: the reader assumes a team that is behind should add people. That frame explains the symptom (too much work) but not the cause (coordination cost). The need is to see headcount as a cost as well as a capacity.

## Step 2: Desire

Truby: the hero's conscious goal, which drives the plot.

Adaptation: the reader's conscious question, what they came to learn. It makes them start reading and keeps them going until the turn.

Diagnostic: can you name the question the reader arrives with? Not the one you want them to leave with, the one they bring. If you cannot name it, the piece will fight their attention.

Illustration: the question is practical, how do I get my team unstuck. The piece answers that on the way to the deeper point, which is that the instinct to answer it with hiring is the problem.

## Step 3: Opponent

Truby: the character or force that wants to stop the hero. Not always a villain; sometimes a system, a circumstance, or an internal flaw. The opponent makes the desire hard.

Adaptation: the conventional wisdom or competing argument the piece pushes against. Without an opponent the piece preaches to the choir; with one, it earns its claims through contrast.

Diagnostic: can you name the position the piece argues against, in its strongest form? A strawmanned opponent makes the argument unearned.

Illustration: the opponent at its strongest is that more capacity means more output, and a team behind on work obviously needs more hands. The piece must beat that version, not a weak one.

## Step 4: Plan

Truby: the hero's strategy against the opponent, structuring the middle.

Adaptation: the piece's argumentative strategy, the order of moves and evidence that builds the case between the opening and the turn.

Diagnostic: can you sketch the arc in three to five sentences? Does each describe a move that advances the argument, or do the moves elaborate the thesis?

Illustration: open on the felt problem, the team drowning. Introduce the hidden cost, every new person adding communication paths. Show a case where adding people made things slower. Land the reframe, capacity was never the constraint. Each move advances; none restates.

## Step 5: Battle

Truby: the confrontation brought to a head.

Adaptation: the central argumentative moment, where the opponent's position is engaged most directly and the argument does its hardest work. The reader is most resistant here; if the piece does not earn it, the rest collapses.

Diagnostic: where is the argument's hardest sentence, the one a resistant reader is most likely to doubt or skim? That is the battle. Are you spending enough words and evidence there, or rushing?

Illustration: the hardest claim is that adding good people can make a capable team slower. It sounds backward, so the battle must land it with enough specifics, the math of communication paths and a concrete case, that the reader cannot wave it off as a paradox for effect.

## Step 6: Self-revelation

Truby: the hero's moment of recognition, seeing what they could not see before. The emotional payoff.

Adaptation: the reader's moment of recognition, where the frame they came in with cracks and the new frame locks in. The self-revelation is what the piece is for.

Diagnostic: can you name the sentence where the reader's understanding shifts? If not, the piece may have no turn. If so, does it have the weight, in placement and prose and evidence, to produce the shift?

Illustration: the turn recasts the problem, that the team was never short on capacity but short on focus, and hiring buys capacity at the cost of focus. It comes after the battle has earned it, and it lands in one sentence, not three.

## Step 7: New equilibrium

Truby: the resolution. Not necessarily happy, but stable; the change is integrated.

Adaptation: the reader's understanding after the turn. The close makes the new frame feel stable, earned and applicable beyond the piece. This is what makes it worth re-reading.

Diagnostic: what does the reader believe after that they did not before? State it in one sentence. If it is fuzzy, the close is not working.

Illustration: the reader leaves believing headcount is a tool with a cost rather than a default answer, and that focus is the thing to protect. They came in equating behind with understaffed. The close lands that without flattening it into a slogan.

## Using the seven steps in the layering pass

Most useful before the prose passes. Outline against them first, sketching the structure as seven moves before drafting; a missing or weak step is a hole. Draft against the outline, making each step present rather than beautiful. Then Williams for clarity, Forsyth at the load-bearing moments, Orwell as the final edit. Applying Truby late produces structural changes that ripple through polished prose; applying it first keeps you from polishing prose into a shape that does not work.

## Things to flinch at

**The fiction-template error.** Forced into strict adaptation the framework produces essays that read as overwrought. The seven steps are a vocabulary for thinking about structure, not a template to fill in.

**The opponent as villain.** In non-fiction the opponent is a position. Dramatizing it produces strawmen. The opponent is a frame, not a person.

**The self-revelation as twist.** It is a recognition, not a plot twist. Engineering a surprise ending produces pieces that feel manipulated. The turn should feel inevitable in hindsight: "of course," not "wait, what?"

**The new equilibrium as moral.** Closing on a tidy lesson is the writing-school trap. The new equilibrium is a stable understanding, not a slogan. Pieces closing on slogans read as engagement-bait; pieces closing on integrated understanding read as earned.

## Scope

Truby's full framework includes character webs, symbol webs, scene weaves, and the designing principle that organizes a story at the highest level. The seven key steps are the load-bearing subset for argumentative writing; the rest is worth reading directly for longer or more intricate work.

## Note to the assistant

After applying these techniques, end with a short note naming the two or three moves that did the most work, plainly, in a sentence or two. Teach as you edit. Keep it brief and high level; a long audit buries the writing it explains. With several craft files in play, write one combined note, not one per file.
