# The function sequence: the folktale's fixed grammar

*Folded from `propp-morphology.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: the order of events beneath the folktale. Not the surface it is told on (the Lüthi file) and not the belief it earns (the Tolkien file).

**The one question.** What job is this event doing for the course of the action, and does it stand where the tale's order puts it?

Propp's claim rests on four findings. Functions, acts defined by what they do for the course of the action, are the tale's stable elements. Their number is limited; he counts thirty-one. Their sequence is uniform; functions may be absent, and absence never reorders the rest. And in structure, all wonder tales are one type.

Source: Vladimir Propp, *Morphology of the Folktale*, second edition, translated by Laurence Scott, revised by Louis A. Wagner (University of Texas Press, 1968; Russian original 1928). The functions, their order, the spheres of action, and the named groupings of the preparatory part, complication, and donor block are Propp's; the remaining groupings, drafting discipline, displacement ruling, and examples are the writer's. Examples are for calibration, not for lifting.

## The function

The tale's unit is not the character or the motif but the function: an act weighed by what it does for the course of the action. An old man gives the hero an iron key; a fox shows him the gap in the wall; a west wind sets him down before the tower. Three tellings, one function: the hero is brought within reach of what he seeks. Who acts and by what means are variables. What the act does is the constant. The same deed can be two functions: a wedding in the last sentence closes the tale, while a widower's wedding in the first sets the misfortune going.

On the page: name the job before you write the scene. If you cannot say what an event does for the course of the action, you do not yet know whether it belongs.

Fails as the jobless event. Incident accumulates because incident is easy to invent, and the tale acquires scenes that advance nothing.

Broken: "On the way he met a pedlar, and they spoke of the roads and the rain, and parted." Held: "On the way he met a pedlar, who sold him a mirror no bigger than a thumbnail." The second event has taken a job; the tale will collect on it.

## The sequence

The order of functions is uniform, the freedom inside it narrow enough to state exactly, and absence everywhere: most tales run a subset, and a missing function never reorders the rest. Propp's numbering, with his first three grouping names and three more for teaching.

- **The preparatory part (I to VII).** Absentation; interdiction; violation; reconnaissance; delivery; trickery; complicity. Someone leaves home; a rule is set and broken; the villain learns what he needs, deceives, and is unwittingly helped.
- **The complication (VIII to XI).** Villainy, or its twin the lack; mediation, which brings the hero in; the decision on counteraction; departure.
- **The donor block (XII to XIV).** The donor's test; the hero's reaction; receipt of the magical agent.
- **The passage and the peak (XV to XIX).** Guidance to the object of search; struggle; branding; victory; liquidation of the misfortune or lack.
- **The road back (XX to XXII).** Return; pursuit; rescue.
- **The homecoming (XXIII to XXXI).** Unrecognized arrival; the false hero's unfounded claims; difficult task; solution; recognition; exposure; transfiguration; punishment; wedding.

On the page: outline by naming the functions the tale actually runs, in order, before drafting a sentence. The outline of a whole tale fits on an index card, which is the point.

Fails as reordering. The reward before the test, the pursuit before the theft, the exposure before the claim: each reads as wrong even to a reader who has never heard of Propp, because the reader has heard a hundred tales.

Broken: "The old man gave him an iron key, and then asked for his coat." Held: "The old man asked for his coat, and he gave it. Then the old man gave him an iron key."

## Villainy and lack

The first seven functions only prepare. The tale proper begins when the villain strikes or the lack declares itself, and the two are morphological twins: villainy creates the want from outside, lack finds it already at home. Mediation brings the hero in, and heroes come in two kinds: seekers, who go after what was taken, and victims, who are themselves taken or driven out. Propp finds no tale in his corpus that follows both. Villainy and liquidation form a pair, the tale is strung between them, and the narrative peaks where the pair closes.

On the page: start the engine early and pick one hero. Everything before the villainy or lack is runway, and runway is cheap to cut.

Fails as the engineless tale, where wonders accumulate and nothing is wanted. Or the double engine, a seeker's quest and a victim's exile braided together, each halving the other's pull.

Villainy: "A dragon carried off the youngest sister." Lack: "The king grew blind, and only the water of life could cure him." Either launches a tale; almost nothing else does.

## The donor sequence

Between departure and arrival stands a stranger, met by chance on the road or in the forest. The donor tests the hero; the hero reacts; the agent changes hands. Three functions, one block, conditional in both directions: friendly donors reward the right reaction, hostile ones are outwitted, and either way the agent is earned by how the hero behaved.

On the page: earn the agent. The test need not be grand; a greeting answered politely is a test passed. What matters is that the receipt follows from the reaction.

Fails as the unearned gift. Equipment handed over without a test is inventory, and a hero who was never tested wins a victory that weighs nothing.

"At the crossroads he met an old man. The old man asked for his coat, and he gave it. The old man gave him an iron key and was gone." Test, reaction, receipt: one block, three functions.

## Struggle or task

The want gets won by one of two routes. In the first, the hero meets the villain in direct combat and defeats him: struggle, then victory, with the branding, a wound or a token, often set between them. In the second, a difficult task is set and resolved: sort a barn of mixed seed by first light, fetch the mate of a slipper no one has seen.

The routes have different seats. Struggle and victory sit before the liquidation of the lack; the difficult task has its canonical seat in the homecoming, where claims must be proved, though tasks turn up elsewhere too. Some tales run one route, some the other, some both across two moves.

On the page: know which route the tale is on before drafting the middle, because the route decides where the hardest scene lands.

Fails when both routes are staged for the same want in the same move: a duel and a task each stealing the other's weight, so neither reads as decisive.

Struggle: "He met the dragon at the ford, and they fought from noon to sundown, and he cut off its head." Task: "Sort this barn of seed, wheat from poppy, before first light."

## The road back and the homecoming

Liquidation leaves the tale only half safe. The road back has its own functions, return, pursuit, rescue, and then the second engine can fire: the hero arrives unrecognized, a false hero claims the deed, proof is demanded, and recognition and exposure settle it. Branding, planted back in the struggle, pays off here: the mark or token is how recognition answers the claim. Transfiguration, punishment, and the wedding close the tale.

On the page: if the tale plants a claimant, fire the second engine and pay off the brand. If it plants none, the tale may stop at the rescue with nothing owed.

Fails as amputation. Modern drafts end at the rescue with the claimant still standing: brothers who rode home ahead with the trophy and are never exposed, a pattern set and abandoned before its payoff.

"He came home in a beggar's coat, and his brothers showed the dragon's heads at court. But he had the tongues in his bag." The proof answers the claim, and now the tale can close.

## Spheres and moves

Propp sorts the tale's people into seven spheres of action, each a bundle of functions rather than a person: the villain; the donor, or provider; the helper; the princess, a sought-for person, counted together with her father; the dispatcher; the hero; the false hero. One character can hold several spheres, and one sphere can pass among several characters. Tales also chain: a new villainy after the rescue does not break the tale, it opens a second move, and the grammar starts again from the villainy.

On the page: cast by bundle, not by headcount. Ask which functions a figure performs, and let one body carry two spheres where the tale is lean.

Fails as one-to-one casting: a distinct body invented for every sphere, so the tale fills with figures who each do one thing and crowd the road.

The fox that tests him at the roadside and carries him over the mountain is one animal in two spheres, donor then helper. The brothers who steal the bride on the road home have opened a second move.

## Relation to the other files

**The Truby file, displaced.** The Truby file is already an adaptation: its seven steps track the reader of an argument. Here the displacement is total on the surface and zero underneath. The surface tale outlines against this file, never against the seven steps, because the tale's figures cannot carry them: a folktale hero has no weakness surfaced, no self-revelation, no new equilibrium felt from inside.

Truby's arc survives on the other side of the doubled armature. The reader still arrives with a frame, still meets a turn, still leaves changed, and the carried meaning can be shaped against the seven steps exactly as that file teaches. The two grammars meet at one joint: **Truby's self-revelation belongs to the reader**, and the tale delivers it through this file's external machinery, the brand, the unrecognized arrival, the claim, the proof, the recognition, the exposure. The hero realizes nothing; the reader realizes everything.

The leakage test: any structural note requiring a figure to see, weigh, or change inwardly is the fiction original leaking through. Externalize it into a function or cut it.

**The McDonald file.** Villainy and liquidation are a pair and the tale peaks where the pair closes. That is a shape, not yet a point. The armature is what the closing is made to prove, and the doubled spine rides this sequence: the same functions that carry the surface tale carry the meaning.

**The Lüthi file.** Same object, two layers, and they explain each other. Isolation is why the grammar snaps together so cleanly; a donor met once and gone is exactly what a sealed episode requires. Depthlessness is why the spheres are bundles of acts rather than characters. Where functions arrive trebled, the Lüthi file owns the count and the Forsyth repetition family owns the mechanics.

**The Tolkien file.** The stretch from liquidation to wedding is where the turn lives. What the turn means is the Tolkien file's ground; this file fixes only where it happens.

## Things to flinch at

**A description, not a recipe.** Propp measured tales; he did not prescribe them. A draft that fills all thirty-one slots has completed an inventory, not a tale. The grammar's best use is diagnostic.

**Absence is not a hole.** The sequence constrains order, not completeness. A tale with no pursuit is whole. When the lack is liquidated and no claim is pending, the tale may stop, and stuffing missed functions into a working tale is padding, not repair.

**The wonder tale is the jurisdiction.** Propp built this from a corpus of wonder tales and said so. Stretched over novels, films, or arguments it goes vague, and that stretching is how the model is most often abused.

**Order is not cause.** The grammar says what follows what, never why. A draft can be in perfect Proppian order and still read as one thing after another. The because lives in the joints, and the McDonald file owns it.

## Scope

Propp's book goes on: the species and varieties under every function, the notation that writes a whole tale as a formula, the chapter on assimilations where one function wears another's clothes, the full distribution of functions among the dramatis personae, and the rules for how moves combine. His later work asks where the form came from; this file uses only his answer to what it is. The structuralist debate that followed, beginning with Lévi-Strauss, is mapped in the supplement to the Lüthi translation. The tale types live in what has become the Aarne-Thompson-Uther catalog.

One warning earned the hard way: online reproductions of the function list are frequently mangled or renumbered, so trust the book. Start with the second edition and read the functions chapter whole.

## Note to the assistant

After applying these techniques, end with a short note naming the two or three moves that did the most work, plainly, in a sentence or two. Teach as you edit. Keep it brief and high level; a long audit buries the writing it explains. With several craft files in play, write one combined note, not one per file.
