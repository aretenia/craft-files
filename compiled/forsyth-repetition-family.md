# Figures of speech: the repetition family

*Folded from `forsyth-repetition-family.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: eight figures that work by deliberate recurrence, building emphasis, rhythm, and memorability. Reach for them when a passage needs weight or a line needs to lodge in memory.

**The shared logic.** The reader has to feel the pattern was chosen. Accidental repetition is an error you cut in revision; chosen repetition is a figure you keep. The line between them is intent, and the reader can tell.

Reach for these at moments that deserve weight: a key claim, a closing, a series that should land as a single unit. Avoid them in dense informational passages, where repetition slows the delivery of facts.

Source: Mark Forsyth, *The Elements of Eloquence* (Icon Books, 2013). Companion to the surprise-family file. Examples are for calibration, not for lifting.

## Anaphora

The same word or phrase repeated at the start of successive clauses. The repeated opening builds momentum and signals the items belong together.

Reach for it with a series of points that should land as a rising set. Anaphora turns three separate sentences into one accumulating argument.

Fails past three or four repetitions, decaying into a tic. Also fails when the repeated phrase is not worth the emphasis it claims, so the structure promises weight the content does not deliver.

"We tested the assumption. We tested the data. We tested the people who swore the data was clean." The third clause lands hardest because the pattern set it up.

## Epistrophe

The mirror of anaphora: the same word repeated at the end of successive clauses, driving a series toward one shared conclusion.

Reach for it when several different subjects arrive at the same verdict. Epistrophe is the figure of inevitability.

Fails when the ending word is not the actual point, and like anaphora it fatigues past three or four.

"The data did not support it. The team did not support it. In the end, the budget did not support it." Three reasons, one verdict. The shared ending makes the conclusion feel forced by the facts rather than chosen by the writer.

## Anadiplosis

The last word of one clause becomes the first of the next, chaining them. The handoff makes a sequence feel causal.

Reach for it when showing a chain: this leads to that, that leads to the next. Anadiplosis performs causation in its structure.

Fails when the links are not real. If "this" does not lead to "that," the chain is sleight rather than argument. Overused it turns sing-song fast.

"A clear brief earns trust. Trust earns autonomy. Autonomy earns the next, bigger brief."

## Tricolon

Three parallel elements in a row. The smallest number that establishes a pattern and the most satisfying to complete.

Reach for it for a memorable triple, a closing list, a claim that wants the rhythm of three. The third element is the payoff slot; it can complete the pattern or break it for surprise.

Fails with a fourth element: three is the number and four is a list. Also fails when the three are not genuinely parallel, or when you padded to reach three and the weakest member shows.

"It shipped late, it ran over budget, and it was exactly what the customer wanted." The tricolon sets the expectation; the swerve in the third spends it.

## Isocolon

Successive clauses of equal length and parallel structure. Where tricolon is about the count of three, isocolon is about symmetry.

Reach for it for comparison and contrast. The matched structure makes two ideas feel weighed against each other, and any difference stands out because everything else is held constant.

Fails as false symmetry, where clauses look parallel but are not, which reads as a near-miss the reader can feel. Mechanical symmetry sounds like an exercise.

"The junior engineer asks how to build it. The senior engineer asks whether to build it." The single changed word carries the whole point.

## Diacope

A word repeated with a word or two in between. The brief interruption, then the return, gives the repeated word emphasis and a small charge of feeling.

Reach for it for single-point intensity. Diacope frames one word by saying it, pausing, and saying it again, which reads as conviction.

Fails when overdone, sounding theatrical, or when the interrupting words are flat and the return has nothing to land against.

"Test, and then test again." Lands harder than "test twice" because the structure enacts the doing-it-again.

## Epizeuxis

The same word repeated immediately, nothing in between. The barest, most forceful repetition.

Reach for it when a single word carries a whole point and should be hammered. The figure of insistence.

Fails easiest of any figure here and reads as shouting fastest. Once per piece is the ceiling; three repetitions is the limit within one instance.

"The priority is retention. Retention, retention, retention."

## Polyptoton

Repeating a word in a different grammatical form. The shared root links the ideas while the changed form moves the thought forward.

Reach for it when you want repetition without the flatness of saying the same word twice.

Fails as the most easily overclever of the family; pushed, it reads as wordplay for its own sake. If the two forms do not sharpen each other, the cleverness is empty.

"A leader who will not lead is just a title." The sentence makes its point in the friction between the noun and the verb.

## Layering them in

After the argument is clear and the prose is clean:

1. **Tricolon first.** Most pieces have a natural triple. Find it and let it be three.
2. **Anaphora and epistrophe for series.** A run wanting emphasis takes a repeated opening; a run arriving at one verdict takes a repeated ending.
3. **Anadiplosis for chains,** where the argument is genuinely causal.
4. **Diacope and epizeuxis for single-point intensity.**
5. **Isocolon for balanced contrast, polyptoton sparingly,** for moments that can carry a little wit.

**The cardinal rule.** Repetition works by being deliberate. The reader has to feel the pattern was chosen, not stumbled into.

## Things to flinch at

**Repetition fatigue.** Anaphora past four units, epizeuxis past three, and the force inverts into a tic. These figures have short ceilings.

**The empty pattern.** Repeating a phrase not worth repeating borrows emphasis the content has not earned. The structure should follow the weight, not manufacture it.

**The sing-song.** Three repetition figures in one paragraph turns prose to chant. One or two per passage; let plain sentences carry the space between.

**Accidental repetition wearing the costume.** If you repeated a word by accident, that is an edit, not a figure. A figure the reader senses you did not intend reads as a mistake.

## When to leave them alone

**Dense informational passages.** Repetition spends time and slows the delivery of facts.

**Where concision matters more than weight.** Repetition buys emphasis with words, and sometimes the budget does not allow it.

**Claims that already land.** Repetition on top of a strong claim reads as overselling something the reader had accepted.

## Scope

Forsyth covers more repetition-adjacent figures than these eight: epanalepsis, symploce, polysyndeton, and asyndeton. The surprise family is in the companion file. For the rest of the book's families and any figure outside both files, Silva Rhetoricae (rhetoric.byu.edu) is a free reference organized by name and function.
