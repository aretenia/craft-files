# Figures of speech: the surprise family

*Folded from `forsyth-figures-of-speech.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: five figures whose effect depends on the reader expecting one thing and getting another. Reach for them when a sentence must land harder than its content alone would manage.

**The shared structure.** Each figure sets up an expectation in the first half and breaks it in the second. The break is the whole job. If the second half is what the reader already expected, the figure has not fired and the sentence is just a sentence.

Reach for these at load-bearing moments: openings, closings, the central turn, sentences doing the work of several. Avoid them in connective tissue, where they call attention to themselves and slow the reader.

Source: Mark Forsyth, *The Elements of Eloquence* (Icon Books, 2013). Examples are for calibration, not for lifting. The figures work when they emerge from your argument, not when imposed on it.

## Paraprosdokian

A sentence whose second half forces you to reinterpret the first. The setup leans the reader one way; the payoff reveals the lean was wrong.

Reach for it at closing lines and section-ending beats, anywhere a sentence should land with a small jolt of recognition. It works when an argument has been building one direction and the close wants to acknowledge a complication without abandoning the point.

Fails when the setup was not leaning anywhere, so the second half reverses nothing, or when the reversal is too clever and the reader re-reads out of duty. Test in isolation: does the first half create an expectation strong enough for the second to break cleanly?

"She was a brilliant manager, right up until anyone disagreed with her." The setup sounds like praise; the break recasts it.

## Zeugma

One verb governs two or more objects, and its meaning shifts between them.

Reach for it when one sentence should compress two ideas into one verb's reach. Especially useful when one object is literal and another figurative: the figure quietly claims both are governed by the same logic without arguing it.

Fails when the registers are too far apart and it reads as a pun, or too close and the shift goes unnoticed. The shift should be visible without being garish.

"He lost his keys, his temper, and any hope of catching the early train." Three objects: literal, idiomatic, figurative. The figurative one carries the feeling; the literal one makes it land.

## Antanaclasis

A word repeats in close succession with a different meaning each time. Same word, different load.

Reach for it when making a point about how the same surface hides different logics depending on context. The figure performs the argument in miniature: the surface stays the same, the meaning underneath changes. Useful for openings that plant an idea without stating it.

Fails when the meanings are too similar and the repetition reads as redundancy, or too different and it reads as wordplay. It works when the gap between meanings is exactly the size of the idea it performs.

"The interview is there to test whether you can test your own assumptions." First as assess, then as challenge. The shift is the point.

## Chiasmus

A sentence or sentence-pair whose second half inverts the order of the first. An ABBA pattern.

Reach for it at thesis sentences and closing lines, anywhere a claim should feel earned by its shape. Chiasmus makes an argument feel inevitable because the structure mirrors itself, and the reader registers the mirror before consciously noticing it.

Fails when the halves rephrase rather than invert, or when the inversion is mechanical and reads as an exercise. It earns its place when the second half means the inverse of the first, not the same thing backward.

"Good writing makes hard ideas easy. Bad writing makes easy ideas hard." Order inverts and meaning inverts with it.

## Antithesis

Two ideas set in deliberate opposition, usually in parallel grammatical structure. The contrast is the figure; the parallelism makes it structural rather than decorative.

Reach for it when an argument has a clean two-sided shape and both sides should land at once. It does the most to set up later argument, putting both halves on the table so the next paragraph can complicate one.

Fails as false antithesis, where the halves are adjacent rather than opposed, or forced antithesis, where the grammar is parallel but the ideas do not pull against each other. It works when the opposition is load-bearing, not ornamental.

"The resume opens the door. The interview decides whether you walk through it."

## Layering them in

In a revision pass, work the figures in this order:

1. **Antithesis first.** Most arguments have a load-bearing two-sided structure somewhere. Find where the central tension lives. It carries the most weight per sentence of any figure here.
2. **Chiasmus at structural moments.** Section openings and closings, the final sentence. Once per piece is usually right, twice is the ceiling.
3. **Paraprosdokian at closings.** End-of-paragraph and end-of-section beats. The reversal is often the closing's whole job.
4. **Antanaclasis where the argument doubles back.** Most pieces use a word in one sense and re-use it in another as the argument develops. Notice those moments and tighten them.
5. **Zeugma sparingly.** The most fragile of the five; small calibration errors push it from argument into wordplay.

## Things to flinch at

**The figure draws attention to itself.** If the reader notices the figure before the point, it is overdressed. The sentence should land as an argument first.

**Correct in form, empty in content.** A chiasmus that mirrors perfectly and says nothing new. An antithesis between things that do not oppose. The figure is scaffolding; the argument is what gets built on it.

**The figure cascades.** Two paraprosdokian closings in a row teach the reader to expect them, which kills both. These figures work by interrupting a pattern, not becoming one.

**The wrong register.** A paraprosdokian in plain expository prose lands as a swerve the reader did not sign up for. The figure should feel like a slight intensification of the prose around it, not a register change.

## When to leave them alone

**Connective tissue.** Sentences moving the reader between paragraphs should be invisible.

**Specific factual claims.** "We shipped on the 14th" does not want a figure. Reach for figures at the moments that interpret facts, not report them.

**Quotations.** Do not lay your figures over someone else's words. Quote clean.

Most of a piece is moments where prose only has to do its normal work. Do not lay scaffolding where no building is going up.

## Scope

Forsyth's book runs to thirty-nine figures across several families: surprise (here), sound, repetition, naming and substitution, inversion, and more. The surprise family is the highest-leverage subset for argumentative writing with structural turns. For figures outside it, Silva Rhetoricae (rhetoric.byu.edu) is a free comprehensive reference.
