# Prose flow and cohesion

*Folded from `williams-flow-and-cohesion.md`, canon V3.3, August 2026. Compiled variant: the source file is master.*

One job: make meaning travel cleanly from one sentence to the next. Reach for it when a draft is correct sentence by sentence but reads as if assembled rather than written.

A process, not a checklist. Five passes, each asking one question and making one kind of change:

1. Character and action: are the grammatical subjects the real actors?
2. Old to new: does each sentence open with the known and end with the new?
3. Cohesion and coherence: do the sentences connect, and does the paragraph hold one point?
4. End-stress: does the most important information land at the end?
5. Concision: is every word earning its place?

Source: Joseph Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*. Each pass below has the same shape: principle, diagnostic, fix, worked example.

## Pass 1: Character and action

A sentence reads as clear when its grammatical subject is the entity doing the action and its main verb is the action itself. When the subject is a nominalization and the real action is buried in a phrase, the sentence becomes harder to parse without becoming more precise.

Diagnostic: find the grammatical subject. Is it what the sentence is about? Find the main verb. Is it the action? If either answer is no, the sentence is misaligned.

Fix: promote the real actor to subject, the real action to verb, and demote or delete the nominalization.

Misaligned: "The committee's evaluation of the proposal resulted in its rejection." The subject is "evaluation," a nominalization. Aligned: "The committee evaluated the proposal and rejected it." Shorter, but that is a side effect; the gain is that the reader no longer has to translate to work out who did what.

## Pass 2: Old to new

Sentences read as connected when each opens with information the reader already has and ends with information that is new. Breaking the rhythm produces friction readers feel but cannot always name.

Diagnostic: find each sentence's first noun phrase. Did the prior sentence establish it? If not, the sentence starts cold.

Fix: restructure so the sentence opens on familiar ground and closes on new. Sometimes a reorder, sometimes a split so a bridge sentence can carry the connection.

Friction: "Customer complaints drove a redesign of the checkout. Cart abandonment was the metric the redesign was built to move." The second sentence opens on new information before circling back. Flow: "Customer complaints drove a redesign of the checkout. That redesign was built to move one metric: cart abandonment." Now the reader's eye and the argument move the same direction.

## Pass 3: Cohesion and coherence

Cohesion is sentence-to-sentence connective tissue; coherence is the whole-paragraph effect of every sentence serving one point. Cohesion is local, coherence global. A paragraph can be cohesive and still incoherent if every sentence connects to the last but the paragraph has no single point.

Diagnostic. Cohesion: can you draw a line from each sentence to the one before and name the connection? Coherence: can you state the paragraph's point in one sentence, and does every sentence contribute?

Fix: for cohesion, add bridges, reorder, or split. For coherence, cut the sentences that do not serve the point, or split into two paragraphs with two points.

Cohesive but incoherent: "The team works from a shared process. Suggestions route through one coordinator. That coordinator also runs the weekly review. The reviews produced several of the team's working rules. Those rules live in a shared document." Each sentence connects; the paragraph does three jobs and develops none.

Coherent: "The process works because one person is positioned to enforce it. Everyone else works behind walls of context they cannot see across; the coordinator is the one with the whole view. Without a real gatekeeper the process would be decoration. With one, it is the team's coordination layer."

## Pass 4: End-stress

English emphasizes what comes at the end. Attention rises through the sentence and lands hardest on the final phrase. Important information at the end reads as emphasized; the same information in the middle reads as buried.

Diagnostic: what is the most important content, and where does it sit? Beginning or middle means the sentence is fighting the reader's attention.

Fix: move the emphatic content to the end.

Buried: "The plan's biggest weakness becomes its biggest strength under this framing, which is the point the review made." End-stressed: "The review made one point: under this framing, the plan's biggest weakness becomes its biggest strength." The attribution is setup; the claim is the payload.

## Pass 5: Concision

Every word is earning its place or stealing it. Words that do not earn their place repeat what nearby words say, qualify a claim into vagueness, or perform a register move the sentence does not need.

Diagnostic: of each word, if I cut this, what would the sentence lose? If nothing meaningful, cut it.

The usual offenders: redundant pairs ("first and foremost"), redundant modifiers ("future plans," "completely finish"), redundant categories ("large in size"), empty words ("really," "basically," "actually," "very"), pompous diction ("utilize" for "use"), and throat-clearing ("it is important to note that").

Padded: "It is important to note that the team has, over the course of the project, gradually developed and refined a fairly comprehensive system for handling coordination." Concise: "The team built a coordination system as the project went on."

## Layering Forsyth on top

Williams produces clean prose; Forsyth makes clean prose land harder. Order matters. Apply Williams first so the prose reads as written rather than assembled, then Forsyth at the load-bearing moments. Applying Forsyth first produces sentences that are clever but assembled. Clean prose earns the reader's attention; the figure spends what the prose earned.

## Things to flinch at

**The clarity trap.** This framework can produce prose so transparent it disappears, which is right for technical writing and wrong for argument. Some friction is good. Use Williams as a default, not a law.

**The character-action fix that flattens voice.** Promoting actors to subjects is structurally right, but doing it to every sentence produces a flat run of declaratives. Vary subjects deliberately; sometimes the abstraction genuinely is the sentence's subject.

**The deliberate old-to-new violation.** Opening a paragraph cold is sometimes right; it forces the reader to lean in. Ask whether you broke the rule for effect or because you did not notice.

**Concision applied to prose that wants extension.** Some sentences do work worth the words they cost. Concision removes what is not earning its place; it does not minimize word count.

## Scope

Williams's book goes further: emphasis through subordination, the rhetoric of long sentences, the ethics of style, and how punctuation interacts with rhythm. The five passes are the core operational moves for a revision pass. For the structure the prose carries, see the Truby file.
